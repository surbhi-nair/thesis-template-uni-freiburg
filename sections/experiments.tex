They were chosen to cover a range of domains and of schema sizes, from 7 tables to 239. We also include five datasets from the BIRD benchmark \citep{li2023bird} in order to perform an ablation study on the effect of competency questions on the modeling. The BIRD datasets are all SQLite databases, and they are modeled with and without competency questions.

\subsubsection{IMDb}

The IMDb non-commercial datasets \citep{imdb_datasets} describe movies, series, episodes, and the people involved in them. They consist of seven tab-separated files, one per table: \texttt{title.basics}, \texttt{title.akas} (alternative titles per region and language), \texttt{title.crew}, \texttt{title.episode}, \texttt{title.principals} (the credits of a person on a title), \texttt{title.ratings}, and \texttt{name.basics}. While the current export from IMDb contains 207.4 million rows, its schema is relatively simpler. In converting the TSV files into a generic KG, we take note of three specific cases:
\begin{itemize}[leftmargin=*, itemsep=2pt]
  \item \textbf{No schema.} The files contain only a header row, without types, primary keys, or foreign keys. Without a schema, every row would be identified by its position and the graph would contain no edges between rows. We therefore refer the documentation provided to declare the primary keys (e.g.\ \texttt{tconst} for \texttt{title.basics}, and the composite key \texttt{(titleId, ordering)} for \texttt{title.akas}) and seven foreign keys inline when calling the converter. 
%   For the same reason, no literal of the IMDb generic KG is typed.
  \item \textbf{Null marker.} Since TSV cannot represent NULL, IMDb writes missing values as \texttt{\textbackslash N}, which we declare as a null marker.
  \item \textbf{Multi-valued cells.} Columns such as \texttt{genres}, \texttt{primaryProfession}, and \texttt{knownForTitles} hold comma-separated lists, which are split into one literal per value with the sub-delimiter option in our converter. Lists of identifiers (\texttt{knownForTitles}, \texttt{directors}, \texttt{writers}) are split like any other column, but not linked to the rows they name. The columns \texttt{types} and \texttt{attributes} of \texttt{title.akas} separate their values with the control character \texttt{0x02} instead, which we did not split.
\end{itemize}
% TODO: confirm the exact sub-delimiter call (columns list) used for imdb_sampled.nt.
The input generic KG we use in our experiments is produced from the IMDb dump using our converter with a sample size of 100, sample threshold of 200 and an FK cap of 5000. Any table with at least 200 rows is reduced to a uniform random sample of 100 rows. Sampling tables independently leaves foreign keys pointing at rows that were not selected. The converter therefore performs one-hop FK repair: it adds back every parent row referenced by a sampled row, up to a cap of 5000 distinct values per referenced column. The result contains 10,421 triples having 700 titles, 200 people, and 100 rows in each of the five remaining tables.

Uniform per-table sampling cannot produce connected data, since foreign-key repair only adds the parents of sampled rows, not their children. \subsubsection{Mondial}

Mondial \citep{may-MONDIAL-report-99} is a geographical database compiled from the CIA World Factbook and other sources. It describes countries, provinces, cities, organizations, and geographical features such as rivers, lakes, mountains, islands, and deserts, and the relations between them, for example which river flows through which province, or which country is a member of which organization. It consists of 47 tables with 193 columns. It's schema has 74 foreign keys, and 30 tables with a composite primary key (a province, for example, is identified by its name together with its country). This makes it the most densely connected relational schema in our evaluation.

Mondial is distributed as PostgreSQL scripts. We convert it into SQLite first and then use our converter script. There are four specific cases that the converter handles separately for this dataset:
\begin{itemize}[leftmargin=*, itemsep=2pt]
  \item \textbf{Composite column type.} Coordinates use a PostgreSQL composite type, \texttt{GeoCoord(Latitude, Longitude)}, whose values are written as tuples such as \texttt{(65,-18)}. Since SQLite has no composite types, each such column is replaced by two numeric columns, \texttt{<Column>\_Latitude} and \texttt{<Column>\_Longitude}, and every tuple is split into these two values.
  \item \textbf{Missing foreign keys.} The DDL declares no foreign keys at all. Mondial documents its referential dependencies in a separate PDF\@. We transcribed them into the converter, which adds them as foreign-key constraints and verifies the finished database with \texttt{PRAGMA foreign\_key\_check}.
  \item \textbf{Encoding errors.} Parts of the data are UTF-8 text that was once decoded as Windows-1252 and re-encoded, such as \texttt{ÃŽle-de-France} for \texttt{Île-de-France}. These are repaired before loading.
%   \item \textbf{Tables without a primary key.} Two tables, \texttt{located} and \texttt{islandIn}, have no primary key, and their rows are identified by position.
\end{itemize}
The input generic KG we use in our experiments is produced from the Mondial dump using our converter, with a sample size of 50, sample threshold of 100 and an FK cap of 1600. Tables with fewer than 100 rows are kept in full (9 tables, mostly small lookup tables such as \texttt{Continent} and \texttt{Sea}). Each of the remaining 38 tables is reduced to a uniform random sample of 50 rows. One-hop FK repair then adds back every parent row a sampled row references, up to 1,600 distinct values per referenced column. The central entity tables are still much larger than the sample size: 430 cities, 529 provinces, 228 countries. The result has 4,043 rows and 26,629 triples. Like IMDb, Mondial is modeled without CQs as well.

\subsubsection{MusicBrainz}

MusicBrainz \footnote{\url{https://musicbrainz.org/doc/MusicBrainz_Database}} is an open encyclopedia of music metadata: artists, releases, recordings, works, labels, places, and events, as well as the edit history of its community of editors. We use the official sample dump \citep{musicbrainz_dump}, restricted to 239 tables, which is the largest schema in our evaluation: 1{,}588 columns and 473 foreign-key columns.
% TODO: one sentence on what filtered.sql removes from CreateTables.sql and why.

The dump consists of tab-separated PostgreSQL \texttt{COPY} files without a header; the schema is shipped separately as SQL DDL\@. We first add a header row to each file from the DDL, and then convert the files to generic KG, where our converter reads the schema directly from the DDL\@ with the following special cases handled: 
\begin{itemize}[leftmargin=*, itemsep=2pt]
  \item \textbf{Keys and references in DDL.} MusicBrainz declares its keys and references not as SQL constraints, but in column comments (\texttt{-{}- PK}, \texttt{-{}- references artist.id}), so the converter reads them from there.
  \item \textbf{Composite primary keys.} Every column marked as \texttt{PK} belongs to the key, not only the first one. 67 tables have a composite key, for example \texttt{(recording, tag)} for \texttt{recording\_tag}. Keeping only the first column would merge all rows that share it into one node so we infer the key from all columns marked as \texttt{PK}.
  \item \textbf{Implicit primary keys.} MusicBrainz ships no separate primary-key definitions, and 114 of the 239 tables declare their key only through a \texttt{SERIAL} column. We infer the key from that column type; without this, every reference to these tables would be dangling. Tables with neither marker get no key.
%   \item \textbf{DDL layout.} \texttt{CREATE TABLE} statements open their parenthesis either on the same line or on the next, and multi-line \texttt{CHECK} constraints have continuation lines that look like column definitions. Matching only the first form silently dropped 25 tables; the continuation lines produced columns that do not exist.
  \item \textbf{Schema-qualified names.} The Cover Art Archive tables are dumped as \texttt{cover\_art\_archive.art\_type}, but referenced as \texttt{art\_type}. The schema qualifier is removed, otherwise these files would receive no schema and all 112{,}142 references into them would be lost.
  \item \textbf{References to absent tables.} The DDL declares tables that the dump does not contain. References to them are dropped.
\end{itemize}
The input generic KG we use in our experiments is produced from the MusicBrainz dump using our converter, with a sample size of 5000, sample threshold of 30,000 and FK cap of 10000000. Because MusicBrainz is far larger than the other databases, we use a much higher threshold and sample size. Tables with fewer than 30,000 rows are kept in full: 180 tables, including core entities such as \texttt{release}, \texttt{release\_group}, \texttt{label} and \texttt{area}, and all type and lookup tables. Each of the remaining 59 tables is reduced to 5000 random rows. One-hop FK repair then adds back every referenced parent row. We set the cap to \(10^{7}\) values per referenced column, so it never limits repair. The result has 1.36M rows and 11.86M triples. Like IMDb and Mondial, MusicBrainz is modeled without CQs as well.

Repair is what makes the sample connected: it brings \texttt{recording} to 95,797 rows, \texttt{artist} to 64,464 and \texttt{work} to 63,142.

\subsubsection{BIRD-SQL Databases}

BIRD \citep{li2023bird} is a text-to-SQL benchmark of 95 SQLite databases from 37 professional domains. Each database comes with natural-language questions, each paired with a gold SQL query, a hint (called \emph{evidence}) that explains the domain knowledge the question needs, and a difficulty rating of \emph{simple}, \emph{moderate}, or \emph{challenging}. We use its curated subset mini-dev \footnote{\url{https://github.com/bird-bench/mini_dev}}, which contains 11 databases, and from these we select five of varying schema complexity: superhero, card\_games, formula\_1, european\_football\_2, and california\_schools.
% TODO: one sentence on why these five of the eleven.
\begin{itemize}[leftmargin=*, itemsep=2pt]
  \item \textbf{superhero} describes 750 superheroes with their publisher, race, gender, alignment, eye, hair, and skin colors, and two many-to-many tables that link heroes to superpowers and to attribute values. It is the smallest database, and the only one in which two tables (\texttt{hero\_power}, \texttt{hero\_attribute}) have no primary key, so their rows are identified by position (\texttt{\_row}\(N\)).
  \item \textbf{card\_games} describes the cards of the trading card game \emph{Magic: The Gathering}. The table \texttt{cards} has 74 columns, and several of them hold lists in a single string, such as the sets in which a card was printed. All four foreign keys reference a non-key column (\texttt{cards.uuid}, \texttt{sets.code}).
  \item \textbf{formula\_1} describes Formula~1 seasons, races, circuits, drivers, and constructors, with results, qualifying, lap times, pit stops, and championship standings. It has the most tables among the BIRD databases (13), the most foreign keys (19), and two tables with a composite primary key.
  \item \textbf{european\_football\_2} describes European football leagues, teams, players, and matches, with yearly attribute snapshots of players and teams. Its table \texttt{Match} has 115 columns: 22 lineup slots (\texttt{home\_player\_1} to \texttt{away\_player\_11}) with their pitch coordinates, betting odds from ten bookmakers, and eight columns (e.g.\ \texttt{goal}, \texttt{card}, \texttt{corner}) whose values are XML documents listing the events of the match. 28 of its 31 foreign keys reference unique columns such as \texttt{player\_api\_id} instead of the primary key.
  \item \textbf{california\_schools} describes Californian schools, with their free and reduced-price meal statistics and SAT results. It has only three tables, but they are wide (49, 29, and 11 columns), with column names such as \texttt{Percent (\%) Eligible Free (K-12)}. Some rows of \texttt{schools} and \texttt{satscores} describe a district, not a school.
\end{itemize}

\paragraph{Competency questions:}
The questions provided by BIRD-SQL for each database are written by domain annotators, they ask what a user of the data would want to know, and, since each comes with a gold SQL query, they have a known answer on \(D\). Since only the BIRD databases come with such questions, they are the datasets used in our ablation study: each is modeled with and without competency questions, by both our approach and the baseline. 

For each of the five databases, we split its mini-dev questions into a \emph{guidance set} of about 30\%, stratified by difficulty, and a \emph{held-out set} of the rest. Only the guidance set is given to the agents as competency questions (Table~\ref{tab:datasets}, column \emph{CQs}). We give the agents the question text only, without the evidence and the SQL query. The held-out questions are reserved for checking whether the produced KGs can answer questions that did not influence the modeling.

The BIRD databases are SQLite files, which our converter converts directly. Primary keys, column types, and foreign keys are read from SQLite's \texttt{PRAGMA} metadata, so no further schema is needed. Beyond the general practices of Section~\ref{sec:approach-generic}, there is one edge case in the BIRD databases that the converter handles:
\begin{itemize}[leftmargin=*, itemsep=2pt]
  \item \textbf{Foreign keys to non-key columns.} The edge of a foreign key points to the IRI of the referenced row, and that IRI is minted from the primary key. When a foreign key references another unique column, as in \texttt{card\_games} and \texttt{european\_football\_2}, the converter first builds a lookup from the referenced value to the primary key of its row, and uses it to resolve each reference. If a value names more than one row, no edge is emitted and the literal is kept. For the same reason, foreign-key repair during sampling looks rows up by the referenced column, not by the primary key; with a primary-key lookup, it would repair almost no references in the \texttt{european\_football\_2} dataset.
%   \item \textbf{Composite foreign keys.} A composite foreign key is reported by \texttt{PRAGMA} as one row per column. The converter groups these rows, so that the foreign key yields one edge to a single row, and accepts it only if it references exactly the primary key of the target table.
%   \item \textbf{Placeholder values.} Some databases encode ``unknown'' as a regular value. In superhero, for example, the race with id 1 is \texttt{-}, a gender is \texttt{N/A}, and unknown heights and weights are \texttt{0}. Following our null handling, these remain ordinary values in \(G_D\): deciding that they mean ``unknown'' is a modeling decision, which we leave to the agent.
\end{itemize}

We evaluate the output of every run on three levels, which correspond to the criteria of Section~\ref{sec:problem-statement}: whether the queries execute at all, how well they model the data, and how usable the result is.
 
\paragraph{Syntactic validity:}
The first level is whether the output CONSTRUCT queries \(Q\) can be executed on the input KG \(G_D\) and materialise a valid graph. We send each CONSTRUCT query of a run to the QLever endpoint of its generic KG, receive N-Triples, and write the union of the results into one file, which is the output KG \(G\). We count a run as valid if every query executes and the result parses as N-Triples. We record which queries needed intervention before they could be materialized, and of what kind.

\paragraph{LLM-as-a-judge:}
The second level is the quality of the semantic modeling and the fidelity of \(G\). An exhaustive manual evaluation of every output, across all datasets, configurations, and runs, would require a considerable amount of manual effort. Instead, we let an LLM agent act as a judge to do an independent evaluation and score the output KGs. Individually, this score is only a rough estimate. Across all outputs, however, the scores indicate which approach generally performs better, and which kinds of issues are most common.
% following the LLM-as-a-judge paradigm \citep{zheng2023judging}. Since we do not use a target ontology, there is no reference graph against which \(G\) could be compared triple by triple.
% TODO: add bib entry zheng2023judging.

The judge receives the generic KG \(G_D\) (as a QLever endpoint and as a file), the queries \(Q\), and the output KG \(G\). It is asked to first explore \(G_D\) and form its own view of how the data should be modeled, then read \(Q\) in full, and then review \(Q\) against nine criteria:
% , which make the criteria of Section~\ref{sec:problem-statement} concrete:
\begin{enumerate}[label=\textbf{C\arabic*.}, leftmargin=*, itemsep=2pt]
  \item \textbf{Entity and class modeling:} the right real-world things become entities, at the right granularity.
  \item \textbf{Vocabulary:} classes and predicates denote domain concepts rather than source names; each predicate has one precise meaning and a natural direction.
  \item \textbf{IRI design:} entity IRIs are minted from stable identifiers, in a domain namespace, with proper encoding.
  \item \textbf{Relationships:} the relationships of \(D\) are links between entities, without \(G\) being a replica of the source schema.
  \item \textbf{Self description:} every appropriateentity has an \texttt{rdf:type}, and entities with a natural name have an \texttt{rdfs:label}.
  \item \textbf{Schema coverage:} the information of every field of \(D\) reaches \(G\).
  \item \textbf{Soundness:} \(Q\) produces only content that \(D\) supports and does not invent new information.
  \item \textbf{IRI consistency:} the same entity gets the same IRI wherever it is minted or referenced.
  \item \textbf{Literal typing:} literals carry datatypes that match their values.
\end{enumerate}
C1 to C4 correspond to the semantic-modeling criteria of Section~\ref{sec:problem-statement}, and C6 to C9 to the fidelity criteria, with C8 covering internal consistency. C5 is added because types and labels are what makes a graph navigable for a human or an LLM\@. Answerability is not judged here, but evaluated with SPARQL queries using the next level of analysis. 
% The criteria tell the judge what to judge, not what to look for: the examples listed under each criterion are explicitly described as a starting point, not as a checklist.

% All queries are logged with a one-line summary of their result, so that every judgment can be traced.

The judge assigns each issue a severity. An issue is 
\begin{itemize}[leftmargin=*, itemsep=2pt]
  \item \textbf{major} if it loses or corrupts information, makes the graph misleading, or makes an important part of the domain wrong or hard to query;
  \item \textbf{minor} if the information is still present and correct, but less clear, less consistent, or less convenient than it could have been.
\end{itemize}

Each severity decision must be justified in one sentence. Each criterion then receives one of five levels:
\begin{table}[!htbp]
  \centering
  \small
  \renewcommand{\arraystretch}{1.3}
  \begin{tabular}{c l}
    \hline
    \textbf{Level} & \textbf{Anchor} \\
    \hline
    1    & No issues. \\
    0.75 & Only minor issues. \\
    0.5  & One major issue, or minor issues so widespread that they affect most of the graph. \\
    0.25 & Several major issues, but the criterion is still partly met. \\
    0    & The criterion is essentially not met. \\
    \hline
  \end{tabular}
  \caption{Levels of the judge per criterion.}
  \label{tab:judge-levels}
\end{table}

The levels of the nine criteria are summed to a score out of 9, which we report scaled to 10. The judge writes its result as a JSON file, with the issues, their evidence and severity, and a rationale for each level, explaining why it chose this level and not a neighboring one. Problems that fit none of the criteria are reported separately and not scored.

The judge LLM that we use is \texttt{DeepSeek-V4.1-Flash}, run as an OpenCode agent, with one judging run per output KG\@. The prompt is identical for all runs, except for a configuration block that provides the path for the input and output files. The judge is not given the prompt of the generation or review agents, and it does not see any of the intermediate queries or trace information that were generated during modeling.

\paragraph{Qualitative analysis:}
The third level is a manual analysis of selected runs. As a preliminary step, we use Protégé to visualize and analyze the resulting KGs. For IMDb, Mondial, and MusicBrainz, we then assess usability with analytical questions. For each question, we write one SPARQL query per output KG. We trace every difference between them back to a modeling decision in the output KG, such as an extra hop through an intermediate node, a cast needed because of a datatype, or an IRI pattern that the user must know in order to filter. In this way, the comparison shows not only which graph is simpler and faster to query, but which specific modeling choice causes the difference. For the runtime measurements, each query is run five times on QLever after one warm-up run, with the result cache cleared before every run, and we report the median of QLever's total query time (computation and serialization).

For the five BIRD databases, the analysis focuses on the effect of the competency questions. For each approach, we compare the run with CQs to the run without CQs on the same database. We identify where the two models differ, for example an entity that is introduced or left out, a field that is modeled differently, or a part of the schema that is no longer covered. We then check whether each difference can be traced back to a specific question. This shows how and where the CQs influenced the modeling, and whether they broadened the model or narrowed it towards what the questions ask.

\subsubsection{Syntactic Validity}\label{sec:results-syntax}

All 25 runs that produced queries yield a valid output KG\@. For the GRASP runs, this holds by construction: every saved query has been executed on the QLever endpoint of \(G_D\) during generation, and only queries whose latest test succeeded can be submitted (Section~\ref{sec:approach-construct}). All thirteen GRASP query sets were therefore materialized on QLever without changes.

For MusicBrainz, the baseline produced no query set at all. In each of five attempts, the OpenCode session kept exploring the generic KG and then stalled without writing any queries. The same happened once for Mondial, where a later attempt succeeded.

The baseline cannot execute its queries on a SPARQL endpoint, and six of its twelve query sets did not run on QLever as written:
% TODO: verify this list against your own notes; it is reconstructed from the output files and the experiment logs.
\begin{itemize}[leftmargin=*, itemsep=2pt]
  \item \textbf{Very wide queries.} The baseline tends to write one query per table, with one OPTIONAL per column. The queries for the widest tables, \texttt{cards} in \texttt{card\_games} and \texttt{schools} in \texttt{california\_schools}, contain up to 72 OPTIONALs in a single query. On these, QLever crashed with a stack overflow in its query planner, or did not finish within 600 seconds. GRASP never hit this problem, since a query that crashes the endpoint fails its test and has to be split by the agent.
  \item \textbf{Predicates built at runtime.} The lineup queries of \texttt{european\_football\_2}  compute their predicate with \texttt{BIND(IRI(CONCAT(\ldots)))} and use the variable in the predicate position, which QLever~0.6.0 rejects with an internal assertion.
  \item \textbf{Unsupported functions.} The IMDb baseline types its years with the cast function \texttt{xsd:gYear()}, which the engines we tried do not support: QLever does not support and rejects with an error, Oxigraph also rejects the query, and RDFLib evaluates it to unbound, so the year triples silently disappear. We replaced the cast with the equivalent \texttt{STRDT(STR(?y), xsd:gYear)}.
  \item \textbf{Unencoded IRIs.} The IMDb baseline mints IRIs from the values of \texttt{title.akas/types} without encoding them. Ten of these values contain the separator character \texttt{0x02} (Section~\ref{sec:datasets}), which is not allowed in an IRI\@. We percent-encoded these IRIs in the output.
\end{itemize}
We materialized the five query sets with the first two problems with Oxigraph instead of QLever, which executes them unchanged, and fixed the IMDb queries as described. None of these interventions changes the modeling of the queries, so the judge evaluates the queries as the baseline wrote them. 

Wherever the baseline chose to test its queries in any of the experiments, it did so with rdflib on the sampled N-Triples file, where a query that crashes, times out, or is rejected by QLever can still run, and where an unsupported cast such as \texttt{xsd:gYear()} does not fail but silently drops its values. Its self-reported verification therefore did not guarantee that the queries produce the triples the agent claims, and some of the output required interventions before it could be materialized.

\paragraph{GRASP versus Baseline (RQ2):}
Since the baseline produced no output for MusicBrainz, only the GRASP run is scored there, and the comparison covers the remaining seven datasets. Without competency questions, GRASP scores at least as high as the baseline on all seven, and higher on six of them; on superhero, both reach 8.9. The mean over the BIRD databases is 8.5 against 7.6, and the GRASP runs contain far fewer major issues: 3 against 16 on the BIRD databases, and 4 against 20 on all seven datasets. 

The difference is concentrated in the modeling criteria. Table~\ref{tab:judge-criteria} shows the largest gaps for
\begin{itemize}[leftmargin=*, itemsep=2pt]
  \item \textbf{Entity and class modeling (C1).} GRASP better identifies the right real-world things to model as entities, with a mean level of 0.80 against 0.65 for the baseline.
  \item \textbf{Vocabulary (C2).} GRASP uses more meaningful predicates and classes, with a mean level of 0.75 against 0.60 for the baseline.
  \item \textbf{Relationships (C4).} The baseline often keeps relationships as literals, while GRASP models them as links between entities. The mean level of C4 is 0.55 for the baseline and 0.80 for GRASP.
  \item \textbf{Self description (C5).} GRASP provides more complete typing and labeling of entities, with a mean level of 0.90 against 0.75 for the baseline.
\end{itemize}
The scores indicate that IRI design, coverage, and IRI consistency are on par. The judge thus finds that both approaches carry the data over equally completely, but GRASP models it slightly better.

\paragraph{Effect of competency questions (RQ3):}
From the scores, it appears that competency questions affect the two approaches in opposite directions. For the baseline, adding CQs never lowers the score: it improves it on four of the five databases (by up to 1.1 points on formula\_1) and leaves european\_football\_2 unchanged, and the mean rises from 7.6 to 8.1. For GRASP, adding CQs lowers the score on three databases, leaves superhero unchanged, and improves only california\_schools; the mean drops from 8.5 to 8.3. With CQs, the two approaches are close: GRASP is ahead on formula\_1 and california\_schools, the baseline on superhero, and they are tied on card\_games and european\_football\_2.

The criteria show where this comes from. For GRASP, the CQs lower entity modeling (C1: 0.80 to 0.65) and vocabulary (C2: 0.75 to 0.65). The clearest example is european\_football\_2: with CQs (G2), only two of the eight event columns become events, while the other six remain XML literals, and the event predicates \texttt{ef:player1} and \texttt{ef:player2} keep the positional source names, although their meaning changes with the type of event. We interpret this as the agent narrowing its model towards what the questions ask, despite the instruction that the questions are a minimum, not the scope of the model. For the baseline, CQs mainly improve coverage (C6: 0.85 to 0.95) and literal typing (C9: 0.80 to 0.90), that is, the questions point the agent to fields and value types that it would otherwise have missed, but they do not improve its modeling of entities.

\paragraph{Schema size and complexity (RQ4):}
MusicBrainz, with 239 tables, has the lowest score of all runs (6.7), with four major issues. Two of them are parts of the schema that are never read: editor collections (981 collections with 4{,}752 membership rows) are not modeled, and the IPI and ISNI identifiers of labels (6{,}624 and 50 rows) never reach \(G\), although the corresponding identifiers of artists do. The other two concern the direct relationship edges, whose predicates are built from the \texttt{name} of the link type. Since these names are not unique, one predicate such as \texttt{rel:part\_of} is used for relationships between areas, between series and works, and between several other entity types. Since they are not directional, edges are emitted in the wrong direction for many link types, for example 8{,}094 edges stating that a series is part of a work. Among the other datasets, the score does not simply follow the number of tables: Mondial, with 47 tables and the densest foreign-key structure, scores 8.9, while card\_games, with six tables, scores lowest among the BIRD databases. What makes a dataset hard is not the number of tables as such, but how far its domain is from its schema, as with the printing/card distinction in card\_games or the events hidden in XML in european\_football\_2, and, for MusicBrainz, the sheer number of tables that have to be covered in one coherent model.

The major issues of the baseline fall into recurring patterns:
\begin{itemize}[leftmargin=*, itemsep=2pt]
  \item \textbf{Structure kept as literals.} In european\_football\_2, both baseline runs keep the eight XML event columns of \texttt{Match} as one string literal per match, so goals, cards, and fouls never become entities and are never linked to their players and teams. GRASP without CQs (G3) models them.
  \item \textbf{Vocabulary repeating the source.} In formula\_1, most predicates of both baseline runs repeat the column names (\texttt{f1:driverRef}, \texttt{f1:qualifyId}), and a single predicate \texttt{f1:position} is used for the finishing position, the qualifying position, the championship position, and the position in a lap.
  \item \textbf{Wrong entities.} In california\_schools, both baseline runs type the ten district-level rows of \texttt{schools} as \texttt{cs:School}, and additionally mint the same districts under a second IRI, which the judge counts as a major issue under C1, C4, C7, and C8. In Mondial, the baseline models each country-specific percentage of a religion as an entity of class \texttt{Religion}, so the same religion is split into one entity per country.
  \item \textbf{Invented content and lost values.} In european\_football\_2, both baseline runs write the index of a lineup slot as \texttt{ef:shirtNumber}, although the source contains no shirt numbers. In card\_games (B3), an invalid boolean cast silently drops three fields of 17 cards, and in Mondial, years are typed as \texttt{xsd:gYear} with the lexical form \texttt{1996.0}, which is not a valid year.
\end{itemize}
Some problems are shared by both approaches and point to the dataset rather than to the approach. In card\_games, all four runs model each printing of a card as \texttt{mtg:Card} and never introduce the abstract card that the printings share, so the list of sets in which a card was printed is attached to every printing. This is the main reason why card\_games has the lowest scores of the BIRD databases for both approaches.

The GRASP runs have few major issues, but they are of a different kind. In california\_schools (G6), addresses and administrators are minted as blank nodes, which are not stable across executions. In Mondial (G5), entities that are only created as link targets, such as 261 provinces, never receive a type.


\paragraph{Limitations:}
Each configuration was run once, and each output was judged once. A difference of one level on one criterion changes the score by 0.28 points, so differences of this size between two single runs should not be over-interpreted. The consistent patterns, the GRASP lead on all seven datasets without CQs, and the opposite effect of CQs on the two approaches, are more robust than any individual difference.

\begin{lstlisting}[language=SPARQL, basicstyle=\ttfamily\scriptsize, columns=fixed]
# G7
?m a imdb:Movie ; imdb:director ?d ; imdb:averageRating ?r ; imdb:numVotes ?v .
# B2
?m a tt:movie ; imdb:director ?d ; imdb:hasRating ?rt .
?rt imdb:averageRating ?r ; imdb:numVotes ?v .
\end{lstlisting}

\subparagraph{Years as integers.}
B2 types years as \texttt{xsd:gYear}, G7 as \texttt{xsd:integer}. The type of B2 is the more precise one, but SPARQL defines no arithmetic on \texttt{xsd:gYear}. In the three questions that compute an age (Q4, Q6, Q8), the natural expression \texttt{?y - ?b} silently returns no value on B2, without an error, and the query has to cast both years through a string:
\begin{lstlisting}[language=SPARQL, basicstyle=\ttfamily\scriptsize, columns=fixed]
# G7
SELECT ?name (MAX(?y - ?b) AS ?age) WHERE { ... }
# B2
SELECT ?name (MAX(xsd:integer(STR(?y)) - xsd:integer(STR(?b))) AS ?age) WHERE { ... }
\end{lstlisting}
A user who writes the natural query on B2 receives an empty result, not a wrong one, and would have to know the datatype to find the reason.

\subparagraph{Code lists as nodes.}
B2 turns genres and credit categories into nodes with their own type and label, G7 keeps them as literals. Nodes are the more expressive choice, since further information could be attached to a genre, but none of the nine questions benefits from it. Instead, B2 needs one more hop to reach the name of a genre (Q1), and the user has to know the IRI pattern of the categories (\texttt{role:actor}) to filter by them (Q6). In Q1, the two additional hops halve the speed.

\subparagraph{Equal cases.}
For keyword search over titles (Q9), both graphs keep the primary title as \texttt{rdfs:label}, so the queries are identical and equally fast. Neither graph defines a class hierarchy, so ``series'' has to be expressed as two classes (\texttt{tvSeries} and \texttt{tvMiniSeries}) in both (Q5), and ``actor'' as two categories (\texttt{actor} and \texttt{actress}) in both (Q6).

The absolute differences are small, at most 54\,ms on a graph of 2.5 million triples, but they are systematic: G7 is never slower, and the largest speed-ups occur in the questions with the most additional hops (Q1, Q6, Q7). Together with the judge scores, this shows that the more compact modeling of G7 is not only judged better, but also makes the graph simpler and faster to query.

\paragraph{Mondial}\label{sec:results-mondial}
The two Mondial output KGs, B5 (baseline) and G5 (GRASP), model the same entities: countries, provinces, cities, organizations, and geographical features, with reified nodes for borders, memberships, and population observations. Both type and label their main entities. The judge scores them 8.6 and 8.9. The two major issues of B5 are that it models each country-specific share of a religion or ethnic group as an entity of its own, and that it types the decimal years of the sample (\texttt{1996.0}) as \texttt{xsd:gYear}, which is not a valid year. The one major issue of G5 is that entities created only as link targets, such as 261 provinces, never receive a type. G5 needs fewer triple patterns on six questions and B5 on two, 47 against 54 in total. In contrast to IMDb, neither graph is better on all questions; each is better where it has materialized a shortcut.

\subparagraph{Folding the country statistics.}
In \(D\), \texttt{Economy}, \texttt{Population}, and \texttt{Politics} are in a one-to-one relationship with \texttt{Country}. B5 keeps each as a separate node that points to the country through \texttt{mond:country}, G5 attaches their values to the country. G5 also adds direct shortcuts from the country to its continents, organizations, and languages, next to the reified membership nodes. Every question that starts from a country therefore needs one or two patterns less in G5 (Q1--Q5, Q8). Q8 shows the effect most clearly, since it combines two of the folded tables:
\begin{lstlisting}[language=SPARQL, basicstyle=\ttfamily\scriptsize, columns=fixed]
# G5
?c ex:independenceDate ?d ; ex:gdpMillionUSD ?gdp ; ex:unemploymentPercent ?u .
# B5
?pi mond:independence ?d ; mond:country ?c .
?e mond:gdpMillionUSD ?gdp ; mond:unemploymentPercent ?u ; mond:country ?c .
\end{lstlisting}
On QLever, the additional joins cost little: G5 is at most 1.3 times faster on these questions, and on Q1 and Q2, B5 is even slightly faster.

\subparagraph{Borders between countries.}
B5 materializes the symmetric relation \texttt{mond:borders} between two countries, in addition to the \texttt{Border} node that holds the border length. G5 keeps only the \texttt{Border} node, which names the two countries as \texttt{ex:country1} and \texttt{ex:country2}. To reach a neighbor in G5, a query has to go through the node, try both sides, and remove the start country. This is the largest difference in the set: B5 is 5.3 times faster on Q10, and 4.3 times faster on Q11, where the path is repeated in a transitive closure:
\begin{lstlisting}[language=SPARQL, basicstyle=\ttfamily\scriptsize, columns=fixed]
# B5
<.../country/F> mond:borders+ ?x .
# G5
<.../resource/country/F> (ex:hasBorder/(ex:country1|ex:country2))+ ?x .
\end{lstlisting}
Where the question is about the border itself, as in Q9 (border length per country), the effect is reversed: G5 reaches the borders of a country directly through \texttt{ex:hasBorder} and is 1.4 times faster.

\subparagraph{Merged feature locations.}
In \(D\), a river is linked to provinces through three tables: \texttt{geo\_Source}, \texttt{geo\_Estuary}, and \texttt{geo\_River}. B5 keeps the three roles as separate predicates. G5 reads all \texttt{geo\_*} tables in one query through a variable predicate, so all three become the same \texttt{ex:province} edge, and G5 adds the country of each province directly to the feature. This gives G5 a one-hop path from a feature to its countries (Q4, Q5), but it removes the distinction between source and mouth. Q13 therefore has no equivalent query on G5:
\begin{lstlisting}[language=SPARQL, basicstyle=\ttfamily\scriptsize, columns=fixed]
# B5
?r mond:sourceInProvince ?ps ; mond:estuaryInProvince ?pe .
?ps mond:country ?cs . ?pe mond:country ?ce . FILTER(?cs != ?ce)
\end{lstlisting}
The information is present in \(D\), but not in the model, and a user of G5 cannot recover it.

\subparagraph{Years and observation classes.}
As in IMDb B2, B5 types years as \texttt{xsd:gYear}, so the yearly increase in Q7 needs \texttt{xsd:integer(STR(?y))} on both years. G5 uses \texttt{xsd:integer} and needs no cast. B5 is still faster on Q7 (21\,ms against 28\,ms), because it has a separate class for the population observations of countries, while G5 uses one class for countries, provinces, and cities and has to select the country observations through their subject.

\subparagraph{Shared nodes.}
G5 links each religion share to one shared \texttt{ex:Religion} node, which avoids the major issue of B5 and is the better model. When grouping by the name of the religion (Q12), however, it costs one hop more than the literal of B5. For the landlocked countries (Q6), both graphs need the same number of patterns.

Overall, the folded statistics and shortcuts make G5 simpler to query whenever a question starts from a country, and the materialized neighbor relation makes B5 faster for questions about the network of countries. The one question that only B5 can answer shows that a more compact model can also lose a distinction that a user needs. Together with the close judge scores, this shows that on Mondial, both approaches produce graphs of comparable usability, with different trade-offs.

G9 needs fewer triple patterns on five questions and more on one, 51 against 69 in total. It is faster on six questions, but slower in total, 742\,ms against 614\,ms, because of Q7 alone; without Q7, G9 takes 219\,ms against 585\,ms. The differences trace back to four modeling decisions.

\subparagraph{Direct relationship edges.}
In \(G_D\), a relationship is a row of an \texttt{l\_*} table, and its type is reached through the \texttt{link} row and its \texttt{link\_type}. G9 keeps this row as a reified node, but additionally emits a direct edge between the two entities, named after the link type. A question about a typed relationship therefore becomes a single pattern:
\begin{lstlisting}[language=SPARQL, basicstyle=\ttfamily\scriptsize, columns=fixed]
# G9
?m rel:member_of_band ?b .
# G_D
?lt lt:name "member of band" . ?k r_link:link_type ?lt .
?l r_lar:link ?k ; r_lar:entity0 ?m ; r_lar:entity1 ?b .
\end{lstlisting}
This saves the most where a question follows several relationships. Q5 goes down the area hierarchy twice, and G9 needs half the patterns and is 12 times faster. On Q1 and Q4, G9 is two to three times faster. Q2 also needs the date on which the member joined, which is a property of the relationship, so G9 has to use the reified node and saves only one pattern.

\subparagraph{Direction of the relationship edges.}
The direct edge always goes from the first entity of a relationship row to the second, and is named after the \texttt{name} of the link type, not after its directional \texttt{link\_phrase}. For areas, the first entity is the parent, so G9 states that \texttt{"United States" rel:part\_of "Virginia"}. The queries for the area questions (Q4--Q7) therefore have to be written backwards, against the meaning of the predicate:
\begin{lstlisting}[language=SPARQL, basicstyle=\ttfamily\scriptsize, columns=fixed]
# G9: the subdivision ?s "is part of" the city ?c
?s rel:part_of ?c ; mb:areaType ?st . ?st rdfs:label "Subdivision" .
\end{lstlisting}
The natural query, \texttt{?city rel:part\_of ?state}, finds one pair of a city and a subdivision instead of 805, without an error. This is the major issue that the judge reports under C4. The answers in Table~\ref{tab:mb-queries} are correct only because we inverted the direction.

\subparagraph{Shared predicates.}
G9 uses one predicate for the same attribute of different entity types, such as \texttt{mb:area} for artists, labels, and places, and \texttt{rdfs:label} for all entities. A query on artists then needs an additional type pattern, and the engine has to scan more triples. This makes the lookups Q3, Q9, and Q11 1.4 to 1.9 times slower than on the table-specific predicates of \(G_D\). In Q7, which combines the area of an artist with the transitive area hierarchy, G9 is 18 times slower. Most of this is caused by the plan that QLever chooses for a transitive path that is joined from both sides: when the closure is computed in a subquery first, the same question takes 30\,ms on \(G_D\) and 37\,ms on G9.

\subparagraph{Dates as one literal.}
\(G_D\) keeps the year, month, and day of a date as three integer columns. G9 combines them into one literal, whose type depends on the precision: \texttt{xsd:gYear}, \texttt{xsd:gYearMonth}, or \texttt{xsd:date}. Every question that computes with years must first cut the year out of the string (Q2, Q8, Q9):
\begin{lstlisting}[language=SPARQL, basicstyle=\ttfamily\scriptsize, columns=fixed]
# G_D
SELECT ?name ((?died - ?born) AS ?years) WHERE { ... }
# G9
SELECT ?name ((xsd:integer(SUBSTR(STR(?died), 1, 4))
             - xsd:integer(SUBSTR(STR(?born), 1, 4))) AS ?years) WHERE { ... }
\end{lstlisting}
G9 also chooses the type by the length of the combined string (4, 7, or 10 characters). A year with fewer than four digits gives a string of a different length, and the whole date is dropped. In the test graph, this affects 26 artists, and 19 rows of the answer to Q8 are missing.

\subparagraph{Equal cases.}
For the release events per country (Q10), G9 links the event directly to the area and skips the \texttt{country\_area} table of \(G_D\), which saves one pattern. For the areas inside each country (Q6), both graphs need one transitive path and are equally fast.

Overall, G9 is simpler and faster to query than \(G_D\) for the part of MusicBrainz that \(G_D\) makes hardest: typed relationships, which need two additional hops per relationship in \(G_D\). It is not uniformly better, however. Its shared predicates make lookups slower, its date encoding forces casts and loses dates, and its reversed relationship edges return wrong answers to the natural query without an error. This is consistent with the low judge score: the modeling of G9 makes the central relationships easy to query, but its errors are of a kind that a user cannot detect from the results.
